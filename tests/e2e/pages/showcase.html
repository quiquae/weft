The road to the water

By A. Kerrigan · 14 minute read

The old house stands at the end of a long road, where the river runs under a stone bridge and the road begins to climb towards the mountain. I walked it first in autumn, when the light was already going by four.

In the morning a woman came down from the village with bread and salt and a heavy book under one arm, and would not say what was written in it. The water was cold that year. A bird sang in the green field beyond the wall, and the light on the water moved like something alive, and for an hour nobody spoke.

Every country keeps a path like this one. It goes past the church and the last house and then it stops caring whether anyone follows. You learn the shape of it with your feet before you learn it with your eyes: the turn by the dead tree, the place where the ground goes soft, the gate you have to lift rather than open.

By evening the wind had turned and the whole world smelled of rain. The sun went down behind the forest. A dog barked somewhere near the town, a horse moved in a field I could not see, and then the night came on and the road gave up its colour and became a grey line between two darker ones.

I have come back in every season since. In winter the snow makes the bridge look new and the river black. In summer the same water is warm enough to swim, and children from the village stand on the wall and jump, and their mothers watch from the road and pretend not to.

What I want to remember is not the view. It is the ordinary fact that a road can be older than every house along it, that people have carried bread and salt and bad news up the same hill for a thousand years, and that the water under the bridge has never once been the same water twice.
