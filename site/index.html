Weft

The web, partly in another language.

The longer you stay on a page, the more of it you read in the language you are learning.

Drag it. Hover any woven word to get the English back — that is exactly what the extension does, on the Standard setting a fresh install gives you. Committed and Monastic go a good deal further.

Why this works

It is not a new idea. The diglot weave came out of Brigham Young University in the 1970s: keep a text almost entirely comprehensible and weave the new language in just above the reader's threshold, so meaning arrives from context rather than from a dictionary.

In a controlled comparison, college learners of Spanish using a diglot reader picked up as much vocabulary as students drilling on purpose-built software — incidentally, from context, and they reported enjoying it more (Christensen, Merrill & Yanchar, 2007). A randomised study of sixty high-school students found a significant advantage over conventional vocabulary teaching after five sessions (Nemati & Maleki, 2014). A US Department of Education IES-funded browser tool built on the same method reported close to 20% vocabulary gain.

The point of that first result is not that weaving wins. It is that it ties — on time you were spending anyway.

Weft's contribution is not the method, which is borrowed and credited. It is tying the difficulty to attention rather than to a fixed setting or a skill estimate. That part is a bet, and it is untested — said plainly here and in the pedagogy notes, which also list what else is judgement rather than evidence.

Function words are never touched

Replacing “the” and “of” destroys the scaffolding you are using to decode everything else, for almost no vocabulary gained. Content words carry the meaning.

Difficulty never goes backwards mid-page

A word you have just worked out does not flip back to English as the page gets harder around it. Rising difficulty only ever adds.

Words you learn stop costing you

Once a word is genuinely known it stays in the target language for good and stops counting against the page's difficulty. It is no longer a demand on you.

You already know more French than you think

A very large part of English arrived from French, and most of the rest shares a Latin root with it. So the word Weft puts on your page is often already inside an English word you use — and the tooltip says which. That is not trivia. It is the difference between memorising cheval and noticing you have known it since the first time you read the word chivalry.

cheval→horsethe root of “cavalry” and “chivalry”
fenêtre→windowthe root of “defenestration”
jour→daythe root of “journal” and “journey”
main→handthe root of “manual” and “manicure”
chien→dogthe root of “canine”
rivière→riverEnglish took the word itself
forêt→forestEnglish took the word itself

Weft keeps the two apart, because they are not the same claim. rivière became the English word; cheval never did, it shares a root with a word that means something else. Saying the second as though it were the first would be a nice story and a false one.

And, if you want it, Latin and Old English

The same device, taken as far as it goes. In Old English the word on the page is usually the literal ancestor of the one it replaced, so you are not memorising a foreign word at all — you are being shown where your own word came from. It is a narrower interest than French, and it is there for the people who have it.

hūs→house
cyning→king
hlāford→lordliterally “loaf-keeper”
steorfan→starveit meant simply “to die”
drēam→dreamit meant joy and music; the sleeping sense is Norse
vacca→vaccineJenner named it after the cowpox he used
mīlle passūs→milea thousand paces
paene īnsula→peninsulaalmost an island

Neither language has a speech voice on any platform, so the tooltip shows a reconstructed pronunciation instead of pretending to offer audio. And because þēod is hard for the thorn and the macron rather than for the word, the letterforms have their own switch — you can read theod until you would rather not.

The screen-time half

Anything that makes Weft easier waits. Lower the density, take a site off the list, drop your level — each is queued behind a cooldown you set in advance. The clock is stored rather than scheduled, so closing the browser neither cancels it nor restarts it. Anything that makes it harder applies at once.

Optionally the cooldown is also gated on a short vocabulary check, drawn only from words you have already been shown. The exit tax is itself the lesson.

Cancelling a queued change is always instant and free. Friction on the way out of a commitment would be coercion; friction on the way into an easier setting is a commitment.

There is a rate-limited panic pass for the moment you genuinely need plain English, because a tool with no escape hatch gets uninstalled and an uninstalled tool teaches nothing. And for people who want the extension itself to be unremovable, Weft generates a browser policy that does exactly that — with the removal instructions inside the same file.

What it will not do

Weft never rewrites a button label, a code sample, anything you are typing, or a screen-reader live region. It ships a never-weave list covering money, mail, credentials and health, checked before anything else — a language tool has no business between you and your bank.

There is no account, no server and no telemetry. Everything is on your machine and it works offline. Weft has access to no website until you name the sites yourself, which is why it installs without the warning about reading all your data.
